AI Visibility: What Is Worth Paying For When a Page-Level Tool Is Free

Metricus Research · April 10, 2026 · 9 min read

You can check what an AI assistant says about your brand yourself, for free, right now. Type your brand name, read what comes back. That takes five minutes. The open question is whether one such check tells you enough to act on, or whether its gaps leave you deciding on incomplete information. This guide covers what a check by hand captures and misses, what a page-level tool shows for one page and what it costs, which is nothing, and when a monitoring subscription is worth paying for.

What a check by hand gives you

Checking by hand works like this: open an AI assistant, type a question someone in your category would ask, and see whether your brand appears in the response. Then do the same on the other major AI platforms. If you are thorough, you write down what you find in a spreadsheet.

This is not a bad idea. It takes about two hours if you test 20 prompts across three platforms, and it gives you something most brands still do not have: a baseline. You will learn whether the assistant mentions your brand at all, how it describes you when it does, and which competitors show up alongside you.

For brands that have never looked at this before, that two-hour exercise can be genuinely revealing. Some discover that an AI assistant describes their product with outdated pricing. Others find that it recommends a competitor they did not know existed. The act of looking, even informally, surfaces information you did not have yesterday.

What checking by hand covers

  • Whether your brand appears at all for key queries
  • How AI frames your product or service in natural language
  • Which competitors AI mentions in the same response
  • Whether the basic facts (pricing, features, positioning) are accurate

If all you need is a quick gut check, this is a legitimate way to get one. The problems start when you try to use it as a basis for decisions.

The variance problem nobody mentions

Here is the fact that makes spot-checking by hand unreliable for anything beyond curiosity: AI responses are non-deterministic. An AI assistant does not return the same brand list twice for the same prompt. There is less than a 1-in-100 chance of getting identical output on consecutive runs of the same query.

This means your single manual check captures one data point from a constantly shifting distribution. You might ask "best project management tools for small teams" and see your brand mentioned. You might ask the same question ten minutes later and see an entirely different list. Both are real outputs. Neither is the complete picture.

The practical consequence: if you check by hand on a Tuesday afternoon and your brand appears, you might conclude that your AI visibility is fine. But you have no way of knowing whether that response represents 80% of the outputs for that query or 5%. Without multiple runs and statistical aggregation, you cannot distinguish between "the assistant consistently recommends us" and "we got lucky once."

What variance means in practice

  • False confidence: Your brand appeared once, so you assume it always appears. It may not.
  • False alarm: Your brand was missing once, so you assume it never appears. It may appear frequently.
  • Invisible competitor shifts: A competitor that appeared in 70% of responses last month now appears in 30%. A single check on either side of that shift tells you nothing about the trend.

This is not a theoretical concern. It is the central limitation of any approach that checks AI output once per prompt instead of sampling across multiple runs.

What a page-level tool adds

A check by hand tells you what the assistant said. It does not tell you what on your page made it say that, and it hands you nothing to change. A page-level tool covers that part, for one page at a time.

Change any passage and the assistant is asked again, so you can see whether the answer moved. You leave with the changes in a file. The Metricus optimizer does this. It is free and there is no signup.

What it does not do

It looks at one page per run. It does not sample a prompt many times to measure how often your brand appears across the whole market, and it does not track anything over time. You can run any URL through it, but the rewrites are only useful on pages you can edit.

What monitoring subscriptions add

Monitoring tools run a fixed set of prompts on a schedule across the major AI platforms and show how often your brand and your competitors appear, week by week, on a dashboard with alerts. That is the difference between reading the temperature once and looking at a month of readings. They cost from about $29 to $489 a month depending on the tool and the number of prompts. What they do not do is show you what on a given page caused an answer, or hand you a rewrite.

When checking by hand is enough

There are situations where a check by hand is the right call and paying for anything would be premature.

  • You have never looked at this before. Spend 15 minutes asking an AI assistant about your brand and read what comes back. That first look is free, fast, and usually enough to decide whether you want to go deeper.
  • You are verifying a single claim. If someone told you an AI assistant says your product is discontinued and you just need to confirm that one thing, ask it. You do not need anything more for one factual question.
  • Your brand is new with minimal web presence. If you launched last month and have a five-page website, AI systems have very little to work with. Build your digital footprint first.
  • You already run a monitoring subscription. If you get weekly dashboard updates across several platforms, another check by hand adds little.

In all four cases, the right answer is either checking by hand or tools you already have. No need to spend money you do not need to spend.

When monitoring is worth paying for

A monitoring subscription earns its cost when any of the following are true. Before any of them, the free steps come first: check by hand, then run your key pages through a page-level tool. Those give you the baseline. Monitoring tells you how it moves.

You are changing pages and need to know whether the change held

A page-level tool shows whether a rewrite moved the answer at the moment you made it. It does not tell you whether the answer stayed moved three weeks later, after the AI platforms refreshed. If you are running an ongoing optimization program and acting on the results weekly, a scheduled prompt set is the only way to see that.

You are presenting to stakeholders

A spreadsheet of notes from an afternoon of checking by hand does not carry weight in a meeting. A trend line over three months, with competitors on the same chart, does. If you need to make the case for AI visibility work to a manager, a client or a board, the record over time matters as much as any single answer.

You suspect the assistant is getting facts wrong across many prompts

Factual errors in AI responses (wrong pricing, discontinued products, incorrect descriptions) cause real damage when prospects rely on those answers. A check by hand spots an error if you happen to ask the right question. A page-level tool shows which passage on your own page produced a wrong answer, so you can fix the page. Monitoring is worth it when the errors keep coming back from sources you do not control and you need to know how often prospects see them.

You want the competitive picture

An AI assistant does not describe your brand in isolation. It ranks, compares and recommends. A check by hand might reveal that a competitor shows up alongside you, but it cannot tell you how consistently or across how many query types. That count, who the assistant favors, for which kinds of questions, on which platforms, is what a monitoring dashboard is built to keep.

You are deciding whether monitoring is worth it at all

The free steps settle this. If checking by hand and a run on your key pages show strong, accurate representation, you may not need monitoring. If they show significant gaps, you have the evidence to justify a subscription and a list of what to track. Either way, you decide with data instead of guessing.

Frequently asked questions

Can I check my AI visibility myself for free?

Yes. Open any of the major AI platforms and type questions your customers would ask. You will see whether your brand appears and how it is described. This takes about 15 minutes for a basic pass or 2 hours for a fuller review across several platforms and prompts. The limitation is that each check captures a single response from a non-deterministic system, so you cannot tell whether that result represents 5% or 80% of what users actually see.

What does a page-level tool show that a check by hand misses?

A check by hand shows what an AI assistant said. A page-level tool shows the answer the assistant gives about one page, the passages on that page the answer came from, and a rewrite for each one, and it lets you ask again after a change. It does not sample many runs per prompt and it does not track your brand over time. Those are what monitoring subscriptions do.

How much time does checking by hand take?

A basic pass through three platforms with 20 prompts takes roughly 2 hours, giving you about 60 data points. To cover more platforms, repeat each prompt for variance, and keep a record of where each answer came from, you would need 10 to 15 hours per quarter. That time is the hidden cost of the free approach: it trades money for a significant part of your workweek.

Is it worth paying for anything if you have never checked AI visibility?

Not at first. A check by hand and a page-level tool are both free, and together they give you a baseline: whether an AI assistant mentions you, whether the facts are right, and what on your key pages causes the answer. That baseline decides every later choice, including whether monitoring is worth paying for.

When should I not pay for AI visibility monitoring?

Skip it if your brand is new with minimal web presence (AI systems have nothing to get right or wrong yet), if you only need to verify a single factual claim (check it yourself), or if you look at AI visibility quarterly or less. In those cases a subscription pays for months of a dashboard nobody opens, and the free steps cover what you need.

How does a free page-level tool compare to a $300/month monitoring subscription?

They solve different problems. The page-level tool shows what one page makes an AI assistant say and what to change on that page. A monitoring subscription shows trend data across a fixed prompt set on a dashboard. Most brands should start with the free steps to establish a baseline. If those show gaps worth tracking over time and you have the team to act on weekly data, monitoring may be the next step. If you check quarterly or less, rerunning the free steps costs nothing.
